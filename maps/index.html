Bringing years of scattered client records into one system

MAPS Community Services helps refugees, immigrants, seniors and families across the Eastside. They brought me in to figure out why their data wasn't working, recommend a better platform, and help them get there.

01The problem

Years of impact, and no system that could show it

When I joined, client information lived in Knack, Google Forms, spreadsheets, paper files and staff members' heads, with finance in a separate system. The two main programs, stabilization and small business support, ran almost independently, so no one could see a client's full path.

This mattered for funding. MAPS reports to about seven grant sources, and funders ask for specific counts like unduplicated people and households by city, income and program. Every report meant exporting everything and filtering it by hand.

Reporting was manual

There was no way to tie a client to a grant or pull a set time period, so staff filtered exports by hand in Excel.

Funder counts were hard

Unduplicated counts by city, age or program needed manual cleanup before every report.

Impact was invisible

Services were recorded, but results weren't. MAPS could say how many people it served, not how their lives changed.

Knowledge walked out the door

When one staff member left, key information about a whole program's participants left with them.

02What I did

From evaluation to handoff, in six steps

There were no set instructions for most of this. Each step shaped the next one, and a lot of it took several rounds to get right.

  1. 1

    Evaluate the old system

    I wrote a Knack System Report over three rounds of feedback, covering what it did, what it lacked and what any replacement would need. I defined the client profile fields from grant requirements and helped compare options like SoPact, CaseWorthy and Salesforce. By mid-June, MAPS chose OMNI.

  2. 2

    Map how casework really happens

    I traced the full casework flow in OMNI, from a new client walking in to a case closing, and confirmed each step with a case manager. The flowchart gives new staff a clear picture without needing prior experience.

  3. 3

    Merge every client list

    I combined client lists from programs like Ramadan Food Box, Eid al-Adha, Senior Connections and Food and Gas Cards, plus years of Knack history, into one template. I standardized names, phones, emails, dates, cities, addresses and demographic fields.

  4. 4

    Preserve the history

    The 13,000+ past cases had only a case ID, a date and a client name. No phone, no email, no birthday. I matched cases to clients by normalized name and resolved a few hundred mismatches by hand.

  5. 5

    Untangle the failed imports

    OMNI rejected 558 rows. I sorted every one by cause, fixed what could be fixed (276 rows approved for import), and organized the rest into tabs with a reason for each, so the developer could decide quickly.

  6. 6

    Hand it off well

    I wrote a summary for the MAPS Board and a detailed handoff document, and every file I touched has a notes tab explaining my process and judgment calls, so the work can continue without me.

03Decisions I made

Small calls that kept the data trustworthy

Identity

One phone number, one person

Phone number became the main identifier across every program. When records conflicted, Knack data took priority over form data.

Privacy

No AI on client data

These are real families' records, so identifying columns were cleaned by hand in Excel, and files were shared only through a secure folder.

Honesty

Flag it, don't guess

When a call was uncertain, I marked the row for review instead of guessing. A "Fix Note" column explains every change I made.

History

Keep programs that ended

I mapped about 12 Knack programs to OMNI categories. Discontinued ones went into a legacy section, so their history wasn't lost.

Quality

Catch what the system missed

OMNI's duplicate check missed obvious matches, like the same name and phone, or the same email with a differently formatted phone. I found and merged them.

Prevention

Fix it at the source

Most problems came from inconsistent entry, so I recommended a short data-entry standard, staff training, and one owner for regular checks.

04Where things stand

One platform, one record per person, and a history that's kept

OMNI now holds 8,471 unique people, along with households, active cases, and clients flagged for housing, disability or income concerns. For the first time, MAPS can see that 1,057 people were served by more than one program. Events and sign-ups live in the same system, and grants are linked to programs, which sets up grant-based reporting.

These counts only reflect what's been entered so far, so they understate the full amount of help MAPS gives.

What comes next

  • A short data-entry standard (phone, full name and date of birth for every client), with staff training
  • One person who owns regular duplicate checks
  • Closing out the last historical cases
  • A community resource map of partner organizations, which I planned but didn't get to build
05Reflection

What I learned

I can work with ambiguity

Taking a pile of messy data and figuring out how to sort and clean it for migration was hard, confusing and very iterative. There were no set instructions, but I figured it out, and it showed me I'm flexible enough to find my own way.

Seeing the whole system

I'm proud that I traced how a system works from beginning to end and turned it into a picture that helps case managers do their jobs.

Real work, real stakes

I got to work inside a real team's back-end CRM. They brought me in to recommend a better path and help them get to a better platform, and I did it.
